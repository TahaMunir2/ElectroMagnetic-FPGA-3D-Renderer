\chapter{The Physical Demonstrator --- User Interface, Control Electronics and Enclosure}

The previous chapters established the physics of the problem, the architecture of the system, the FDTD solver that performs the computation, and the renderer that turns its output into a picture. This chapter describes the part of the project the user actually touches: the physical demonstrator. It explains how a person interacts with the simulation through a touch panel, knobs and switches; how those actions are sensed, conditioned and digitised by an analog front end and a microcontroller; how the resulting data is delivered to the rest of the system; and how all of it is housed in a purpose-built, 3D-printed console. It also explains the physics of the two live experiments the demonstrator performs, and closes with the design history that led to the final, deliberately simple hardware.

\section{What the demonstrator does, and the physics behind it}

The motivating difficulty of the whole project is that electromagnetic fields are invisible and abstract: a student can be told that a potential satisfies Laplace's equation, or that a wave diffracts around an obstacle, but the equations rarely become intuitive. The demonstrator's job is to make the fields tangible --- to let a person \emph{poke} a field with their finger or a probe and watch it respond in real time. It does this through two complementary experiments, selected by a single front-panel switch.

\textbf{Experiment one --- measuring a real electrostatic field.} The first mode turns a hand-made sheet of resistive material into a small analog computer. A thin flexible board is airbrushed with conductive carbon paint to a controlled sheet resistance, a small copper-tape electrode at its centre is driven to $+5$\,V, and all four edges are tied to ground. Current spreads outward through the resistive film, and the steady-state voltage across the sheet settles into the smooth, bowl-shaped distribution that is the unique solution of Laplace's equation for those boundary conditions. In other words, the sheet \emph{physically solves} the same electrostatics problem the computer is asked to solve. A fine probe touched to any point reads the local potential there, which falls continuously from roughly five volts at the centre to nearly zero at the grounded rim. The demonstrator captures both \emph{where} the probe is and \emph{what voltage} it reads, hands the voltage to the solver as a fixed boundary value, and the on-screen simulation relaxes to the very same field the paper is computing by hand. Seeing the measured analog field and the digital reconstruction agree is the payoff of this mode.

\textbf{Experiment two --- playing with waves.} The second mode runs the full time-dependent simulation. Here the same touch surface becomes a drawing canvas: a stylus press plants a wave source, drops a reflecting wall, or erases part of the scene, depending on a mode switch and a clear button. The wave then propagates, reflects, interferes and diffracts live on the screen. A dedicated knob scales the speed of propagation in the medium, so the user can slow light down, speed it up, or make the medium lossy and watch the wave decay. Where the first experiment is about a static field in equilibrium, the second is about dynamics, and together they cover the two faces of the subject.

\section{The user interface}

Everything the user can adjust is laid out on a single sloping front face so the controls can be reached and read at a glance. The interface comprises one touch surface, four switches, a push-button and seven rotary knobs.

The \textbf{touch panel} is the primary input and serves both experiments. The \textbf{mode switch} chooses between the two experiments; a \textbf{2D/3D switch} chooses whether the field is drawn flat or as a rendered surface; a \textbf{wall/source switch} chooses what a stylus press does in the wave experiment; and a momentary \textbf{clear button} wipes the scene (and doubles as a way to trigger touch-panel recalibration when held during power-up). The seven \textbf{knobs} cover source amplitude, the conductivity of the medium (which sets wave speed), a field-type selector, and four that steer the 3D camera view: yaw, pitch, zoom and vertical scale.

The field-type selector deserves a note, because it is a small example of designing around the parts actually available. It chooses which quantity is displayed --- the electric field, the magnetic field, or the Poynting vector that represents energy flow --- which is naturally a three-position choice. No suitable three-position switch was on hand, so an ordinary knob is used instead, and its rotation is split into three equal zones in software. The result behaves exactly like a three-way switch using a component we already had.

A guiding principle for the whole panel is that the controls are \emph{normalised}: each knob simply reports a fraction of its full travel, and the meaning of that fraction is interpreted downstream. The one exception is the probe in the first experiment, which reports a genuine measured voltage rather than an abstract fraction. Keeping everything else dimensionless makes the same controls reusable across both experiments without rewiring.

\section{Power supply and analog conditioning}

The entire control subsystem runs from a single five-volt supply taken from the USB connection it already shares with the FPGA board, which also ties the two grounds together. From that one rail the electronics derive everything they need.

The most important decision in the power design concerns the \emph{reference} voltage against which the knobs are measured. The microcontroller provides its own 3.3\,V output pin, but under load that pin sags to roughly 2.9 volts and cannot supply much current. If the knobs were referenced to it, their full rotation would no longer correspond to the analog-to-digital converter's full range, so part of every knob's travel would be wasted and the readings would drift as the load changed. To avoid this, a \textbf{clean, buffered 3.3\,V control rail} is generated independently: the stiff 5\,V rail is divided down to about 3.3\,V by a resistor pair, and that divided voltage is buffered by an operational amplifier wired as a unity-gain follower, producing a low-impedance rail that holds steady regardless of how many knobs are drawing from it. Every knob's top end is connected to this buffered rail, so each knob's full sweep maps precisely onto the converter's full scale. The switches need no such care --- they simply connect their pin to ground when pressed, using the microcontroller's internal pull-up resistors to define the released state.

A single, inexpensive \textbf{dual operational amplifier} does two jobs at once, which is a tidy economy. One half is the unity-gain follower that buffers the control rail just described. The other half is the \textbf{probe buffer} for the first experiment. The probe must read the voltage on the resistive sheet without disturbing it, because drawing any appreciable current would distort the very field being measured. The op-amp presents an enormous input impedance --- effectively infinite compared with the sheet --- so it senses the local potential while drawing almost nothing. Its output is then scaled down by a resistor divider so that the probe's zero-to-five-volt range fits safely inside the converter's zero-to-3.3-volt window, and a large resistor to ground ensures the input reads a definite zero rather than floating when the probe is lifted off the sheet. Figure~\ref{fig:afe} shows both halves of the front end as a block diagram.

\section{The microcontroller and the control link}

At the centre of the subsystem is an \textbf{ESP32 microcontroller}. It was chosen because it combines a built-in multi-channel analog-to-digital converter, plenty of input/output pins, a hardware serial port, and a familiar development environment, all on a small, cheap module. Its sole responsibility is to \emph{read} the controls and \emph{report} them; it performs none of the physics itself, acting purely as an input concentrator.

Two rules shape how the chip is used. First, its wireless radio is kept switched off throughout, because enabling it would disable a whole bank of the analog inputs that the design relies on. Second, although some of the chip's pins can output true analog voltages, none of that capability is used; the touch panel is driven with plain digital highs and lows, and any small imperfection is absorbed later by calibration. These two rules keep the design within the chip's safe, well-behaved set of pins.

The chip is almost fully occupied. Of roughly two dozen usable pins, twenty are in use, allocated as in Table~\ref{tab:pins}.

Filling every available input means a couple of the controls sit on pins that carry minor caveats --- one knob uses an input-only pin, and the wall switch and clear button sit on pins that the chip samples briefly at start-up --- but each of these was checked to be harmless in its idle state, so the board still boots reliably. The design has essentially no spare capacity, which is itself a finding: it is at the practical limit of what a single chip of this class can do, and that observation drove much of the design evolution discussed later.

The firmware runs a simple, fixed cycle about fifty times a second, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:loop}. On each pass it notes the positions of the mode and display switches, checks whether the panel is being touched and, if so, measures the touch coordinates; it then reads the seven knobs, the field-type zones, the probe (in the first experiment) and the remaining switches; finally it assembles all of this into a compact, fixed-size message, appends an integrity check, and sends it down the serial link. During development a running commentary of the live readings is printed to a connected computer so the hardware can be watched as it is exercised.

When no valid touch is present, the position field is sent as zero and a flag tells the receiver not to place anything that frame, so a lifted finger never plants a stray source or boundary.

One subtlety of the final design is worth highlighting. The microcontroller cannot reach the renderer over the network directly, because that would require the wireless radio that has been deliberately disabled. Instead it sends its packet over the wired serial link to the processor on the FPGA board, and that processor passes the same data onward to the renderer. The control data therefore travels by wire out of the console and only joins the network once it is safely inside the main system.

\section{The touch panel: sensing and calibration}

The touch panel is the most delicate part of the demonstrator, and getting clean, trustworthy coordinates out of it absorbed more design effort than any other single element.

\textbf{How a resistive panel works.} The panel is a four-wire resistive type: two transparent conductive sheets held a hair apart, each with electrodes along two opposite edges. Pressing the surface makes the two sheets touch at the contact point. Reading a position is a two-step electrical trick. To find the horizontal coordinate, a voltage is applied across one sheet so it behaves like a uniform ramp from one edge to the other; the other sheet, left floating, picks up the voltage at the contact point, and that voltage reveals how far across the press is. To find the vertical coordinate, the roles of the two sheets are swapped. A press is detected, before either measurement, by holding one electrode low and watching another through a pull-up resistor: untouched it reads high, touched it is pulled low.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\figplaceholder{diagram of the four-wire panel: the two conductive layers, the edge electrodes, and the two measurement phases showing how the horizontal and vertical positions are read in turn}
\caption{Reading a four-wire resistive panel. \emph{(Insert diagram.)}}
\label{fig:touch}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Reading it cleanly.} Each axis is measured on its own dedicated sensing input, separate from the pin that drives it. This matters because the point being sensed is a high-impedance node whose voltage depends on position, and any leakage on the sensing pin would bend the otherwise straight relationship between position and voltage. By sensing on the chip's cleanest, lowest-leakage inputs and reading them with the converter's built-in millivolt calibration, the panel returns a satisfyingly linear coordinate. On top of that, every reading is filtered: the touch test takes the median of three quick samples to throw out a single glitch, and each coordinate is the trimmed average of a dozen samples with the highest and lowest few discarded, which rejects both sharp spikes and slow drift. A short settling pause after each electrical reconfiguration lets the panel stabilise before it is sampled.

\textbf{Fixing orientation in software, never by rewiring.} A standing discipline across the whole subsystem is that if an axis comes out reversed, or the two axes come out swapped because the panel is rotated relative to its housing, the fix is made in software, not by re-soldering. The panel's longer edge and shorter edge are mapped to the appropriate screen axes in code, and a mirrored axis is simply flipped numerically. This keeps the documented wiring authoritative and avoids a whole category of confusing assembly errors.

\textbf{Calibration.} A resistive panel never quite reaches its theoretical extremes at the physical corners --- the corners read somewhat short of the true zero and full values --- so the firmware has to learn the real range before its numbers mean anything. It does this with a quick \textbf{two-corner calibration}: the user presses one corner and then the opposite corner, and the firmware records the smallest and largest readings on each axis from those two firm presses. Only firm presses are accepted, so a light accidental brush cannot corrupt the range. The learned calibration is then stored in the chip's non-volatile memory and reloaded automatically every time the demonstrator is powered on, so it only has to be done once. If it ever needs redoing --- a different panel, or a knock to the setup --- the user simply holds the clear button while switching on, or issues a one-key command from a connected computer, and the routine runs again. As a safeguard, a stored calibration is only trusted if it spans a sensible range; a nonsensical one is ignored and a fresh calibration is requested.

Once calibrated, each press is converted in two stages: first to a proportioned full-scale value that keeps the same number of counts per millimetre on both axes, so a square gesture reads as square rather than stretched; and then to the nearest cell of the display grid. The packet carries that grid cell directly, which means the rest of the system can use the touch position immediately without any further scaling.

\section{Enclosure and mechanical design (box modelling)}

A demonstrator meant to be handled by strangers at a showcase needs a real housing, not a loose breadboard, so a custom enclosure was modelled and 3D-printed. The mechanical design was developed in parallel with the electronics and went through the same drive toward simplicity.

\textbf{The concept: a cover, not a box.} Rather than a conventional clamshell that the electronics are screwed inside, the enclosure is a single \textbf{open-bottomed shell} --- imagine an upturned tray --- that simply lowers over the populated baseboard on which the circuitry and the FPGA board are mounted. This choice has several practical virtues. During development the whole cover lifts straight off to expose everything underneath, with no fasteners to undo. In use it presents the user with one clean, continuous top surface and no visible screws. And because it is a cover rather than a sealed case, there is nothing to trap heat or block connectors. The trade-off is that the cover provides no internal mounting of its own; the boards are fixed to the baseboard beneath, and the cover only has to register correctly over them.

\textbf{Overall form and proportions.} The cover is a broad, shallow shell roughly \textbf{318\,mm wide, 172\,mm deep and 55\,mm tall} --- wide enough to spread the controls comfortably along the front while leaving the back half for the panel and the FPGA board. The vertical walls are \textbf{3\,mm} thick for stiffness, and the top plate is a more generous \textbf{5\,mm}, because that top surface has to carry the recessed panel and every control hole without flexing when the knobs and switches are pushed and turned.

\textbf{The top face, zone by zone.} The top surface is organised into three working areas, and describing each in turn is the clearest way to picture the whole model.

The \textbf{touch-panel window} sits at the back-left. It is not a simple hole but a stepped opening, because the panel has to sit flush and be held captive. The top plate is rebated to form a shallow recess, a little over a couple of millimetres deep and very slightly larger than the panel itself, into which the panel drops. Around the inside of that recess a narrow shelf --- a \textbf{lip a few millimetres wide} --- is left standing, so the panel rests on the lip from below while a smaller through-opening in the centre exposes the panel's active touch area. The panel is thus trapped between the lip beneath and its own bezel above, sitting perfectly flush with the surrounding surface, while its thin connector tail passes down through into the cavity. In the first experiment, the conducting paper sheet is laid into this same recess on top of the panel.

The \textbf{FPGA bay} sits at the back-right: a generous rectangular cut-through, roughly 120 by 90 millimetres, that leaves the FPGA board completely uncovered. This is deliberate. The board's video output, its USB connections, its buttons and its status lights all need to remain reachable and visible during a live demonstration, and leaving it in an open bay also means nothing covers it to trap heat. The cover frames the board rather than hiding it.

The \textbf{control strip} runs along the front of the top face: a single straight row of eleven holes, sized to their parts. Seven of them, each about seven millimetres across, take the threaded bushings of the knobs; three smaller holes take the miniature toggle switches; and one larger hole, about twelve millimetres, takes the clear push-button. They are spaced evenly between fixed margins on the left and right. Reading from left to right the row is: the four camera knobs (yaw, pitch, zoom and vertical scale), then amplitude, conductivity and field-type, then the mode, display and wall switches, and finally the clear button at the end.

Finally, a \textbf{cable slot} --- a rectangular opening about 40 by 22 millimetres --- is cut into the back wall so the power, serial and video leads can exit the console tidily.

\textbf{Two companion frames.} Besides the main cover, two small parts are printed that turn out to be the key to making the single-panel idea practical. The first is a slim \textbf{panel frame}, a bezel that lays over the edge of the touch panel to clamp it neatly into its recess and hide the panel's raw border. The second is a \textbf{paper frame} that holds the hand-painted conducting sheet flat and correctly positioned over the panel during the first experiment; without it the floppy painted sheet would curl and lose even contact with the panel underneath. Because the same window can host either the bare panel or the panel-plus-framed-sheet, these two frames are what let one physical opening serve both experiments.

\textbf{A parametric model.} The enclosure was not drawn as a one-off shape but built \textbf{parametrically}: every meaningful dimension --- wall thickness, panel size, recess depth, lip width, the diameter of each kind of hole, the size and position of the FPGA bay, the control spacing and the overall envelope --- is a named value, and everything else in the model is calculated from those values. The practical benefit is that the design can be corrected or resized by changing a single number rather than redrawing. If a batch of knobs turns out to need a slightly larger hole, or a thicker panel needs a deeper recess, one edit regenerates a fully consistent model. The geometry was kept in more than one modelling tool so it could be opened and edited in whichever package was to hand, with all of them sharing the same set of dimensions.

\textbf{Printing strategy.} The cover is designed to be printed \textbf{upside-down, with its top face flat on the build plate.} This is a deliberate manufacturing choice: printed in that orientation, every opening --- the panel window, the FPGA bay, the control holes and the cable slot --- is formed by walls that rise straight up off the plate with nothing overhanging, so the print needs no support material at all and the most visible surface, the top face, comes out as the smooth plate-side finish. The parts are suited to ordinary desktop materials such as PLA or PETG, printed in fine layers with a few perimeters for rigidity.

\textbf{Fitting a large object on a small printer.} At 318 millimetres wide the cover is bigger than the bed of most desktop printers, which typically top out around 220 to 256 millimetres. The parametric model offered several ways out --- print on a larger machine, or shrink the FPGA bay or the margins to narrow the whole thing --- but the route actually taken was to \textbf{split the cover into two halves} that print separately and join along a seam, so the full-size console can be produced on a standard printer. This is why the finished enclosure is made up of two main box parts together with the two frames described above.

\textbf{Assembly and limitations.} Putting it together is straightforward: the electronics and the FPGA board are fixed to the baseboard; the knobs and switches are fastened into their front-row holes, where their threaded bushings clamp onto the top plate; the touch panel drops into its recess and is held by the panel frame; the two cover halves are joined; and the assembled cover is lowered over the baseboard, with the cables led out through the back slot while the FPGA remains exposed in its bay. The design does carry some honest limitations, each a consequence of keeping it a simple cover: the hole sizes are nominal and may need fine adjustment to a particular batch of parts; it assumes a particular panel thickness; it provides one panel window, which is exactly what the final single-panel design needs but no more; and it has no internal posts or screw bosses, so all real mounting happens on the baseboard rather than on the cover itself.

\section{Design evolution: how the hardware reached its final form}

The demonstrator did not arrive at its final shape in one step. It passed through three distinct hardware iterations, and the path between them is itself instructive, because each step traded a piece of complexity for simplicity without giving up what mattered.

\textbf{First iteration --- two separate panels, maximum features.} The original design used \textbf{two} touch panels, one dedicated to each experiment, each wired straight to the microcontroller. It carried the full set of seven knobs, and it squeezed the mode and display selections onto a single shared input through a small resistor-ladder encoding. Calibration was manual: a helper program was used to read each panel's raw extremes, and those numbers were typed into the firmware by hand. This design had the richest feature set and the fewest extra components, but it was uncomfortable: it pushed the microcontroller's pin count to its limit, the two panels and the resistor ladder added wiring and analog complexity, and the way each panel reused one pin for both driving and sensing slightly bowed its coordinate accuracy. Calibration by hand was slow and easy to get wrong.

\textbf{Second iteration --- sharing one set of wiring through a switch.} The second design attacked that complexity. Since only one experiment runs at a time, only one panel is ever live, so a small \textbf{analog switch chip} was added to route a single, cleaner set of panel wiring to whichever panel the current mode selects. This version moved each panel to a tidier scheme with separate driving and sensing connections, which straightened out the coordinates, and it replaced the resistor-ladder mode encoding with two plain switches. It also introduced automatic calibration, removing the type-it-in-by-hand step. The cost of fitting all this within the pin budget was that the four camera-view knobs had to be dropped, so the 3D view became fixed, and the design now depended on an extra component and its own power arrangements. It was cleaner and more linear than the first, but still fundamentally a two-panel system --- physically larger and mechanically more involved --- and its automatic calibration had to be redone at every power-up.

\textbf{Final iteration --- one panel for everything.} The decisive simplification came from the observation made earlier: because the two experiments never run at once and both only need a touch position, \textbf{one panel can serve both.} In the first experiment the conducting sheet simply lies on top of that single panel. This removed the second panel, removed the analog switch, and removed any need to coordinate between two input paths --- all in one stroke. The final design keeps the clean, linear sensing of the second iteration, brings back the full set of seven knobs, and upgrades calibration to the persistent, two-press routine described earlier, which survives power cycles and can be redone in seconds. It also does a little more of the work itself, handing the rest of the system a touch position already expressed as a grid cell. The one price paid is that fitting the full feature set leaves the microcontroller with essentially no spare pins, which is an acceptable and well-understood limit rather than a problem.

\section{Summary}

The physical demonstrator is the part of the project that makes the invisible physics graspable. Through a single touch surface, a handful of switches and seven knobs, a user can either measure a real electrostatic field on a conducting sheet and watch the simulation agree with it, or paint sources and walls and watch waves propagate. Behind that interface sits a carefully conditioned analog front end --- a stable buffered reference rail and a high-impedance probe amplifier --- feeding a single microcontroller that digitises every control and streams it, in a compact and checksummed packet, by wire into the rest of the system. The touch panel, the fussiest element, is made trustworthy through linear sensing, heavy filtering, a strict discipline of correcting orientation in software, and a quick calibration that the demonstrator remembers between sessions. All of it is housed in a purpose-modelled, 3D-printed cover whose every dimension is parametric and which prints without supports and splits to fit an ordinary printer. The journey from a complex two-panel prototype to the final one-panel console --- which restored every feature while shedding both an entire second panel and an extra switching chip --- is the clearest illustration of the design philosophy that runs through the whole subsystem: do the most for the user with the least to go wrong.
